## 3. Specialist-book baseline

### SOURCE-CONFIRMED — Michael Beitl, Alarm am Arm

#### ARSA Blind Alarm

Historical text:
- ARSA is described as producing a blind alarm wristwatch around **1956** using **AS 1475**.
- the user could open the crystal cover with a pusher integrated in the winding crown
- both current time and alarm time could be felt
- alarm time was set using the alarm crown around 2 o'clock

Catalog specimen:
- ARSA Alarm / Blindenwecker
- chrome-plated metal case
- screw back
- **AS 1475**
- specimen dated **1958**
- raised tactile markers
- robust tactile hands
- no seconds hand
- crystal opened via pusher in crown around 4 o'clock
- described as rare and unworn

**Chronology rule:** do not collapse “ARSA Blind Alarm existed around 1956” into “introduced in 1958.” The 1958 date belongs to the catalog specimen; the historical narrative places ARSA production around 1956.

#### A. Schild prototype

- A. Schild prototype of a blind alarm
- stainless case
- **AS 1475**

Current evidence shows only that A. Schild made a prototype. It does **not** prove that this prototype was the direct design ancestor of ARSA's commercial model.

#### Enicar Blind Alarm

- ca. **1957**
- stainless case
- screw back
- opening crystal
- silvered tactile dial
- tactile hands
- **AS 1475, 17 jewels**
- pusher in crown around 4 o'clock
- no seconds hand
- Beitl explicitly notes a similar ARSA blind alarm
- a gold-plated variant is also listed

#### BEAT / Friedli-Frères

- blind alarm
- chrome-plated metal case, approx. 33.8 mm
- screw back
- tactile markers / tactile hands
- **AS 1475**
- unlike the more usual 4 o'clock crown-pusher layout, opening control is external at 6 o'clock
- made by Friedli-Frères SA
- AS movement signed by Friedli-Frères

#### AFB entry

Beitl describes:
- AFB, American Federation for Blinds (name as printed in the book)
- blind alarm wristwatch
- gold-plated metal case
- screw steel back
- tactile hour dots
- robust tactile minute/hour hands
- hinged glass cover
- opening pusher in crown around 4 o'clock
- **AS 1475**
- around **1960**
- commissioned from ARSA for the blind organization
- described as identical to ARSA's own blind alarm model

**Conflict:** the official organization is **American Foundation for the Blind (AFB)**. No independent evidence has yet been found for a separate relevant organization named “American Federation for Blinds.” Treat Beitl's organization-name wording as a probable error, but do not use that to automatically validate every other detail of the ARSA order story.

### SOURCE-CONFIRMED — Michael Philip Horlbeck, The Alarm Wristwatch

Blind-alarm design description:
- blind alarm watches use touch-reading of hands
- crown shapes are differentiated to avoid accidental confusion
- the 4 o'clock time crown can include the button that opens the watch
- the user can feel both current time and set alarm time
- hour/minute/alarm hands require deliberately distinct tactile shapes
- no seconds hand is generally used because it would interfere with tactile reading and may stop the movement when touched

Enicar special model:
- AS 1475
- opening lunette
- both clock and alarm times can be felt
- illustrated special model built for the **1964 Lausanne state exposition**
- attribution to Enicar is based on case/base form rather than a visible Enicar signature

### Case / opening mechanism conclusion

Current evidence does **not** justify saying ARSA, Enicar and BEAT all used the same complete case.

More defensible model:

- common functional architecture: alarm movement + tactile dial/hands + opening front + tactile access
- ARSA / AFB appear especially close and Beitl explicitly calls them identical
- Enicar solves a similar problem but external design differs
- BEAT differs materially by moving the opening control to 6 o'clock
- A. Schild prototype exterior differs from ARSA commercial example in the book

Therefore:

> **Shared architecture is supported; a universal common-case supplier is not yet proven.**

### Design-origin question — OPEN

Not proven:

- A. Schild created the blind-alarm concept and ARSA commercialized it
- ARSA originated the complete design
- AFB commissioned a new design rather than ordering / rebadging an ARSA model
- one case supplier provided ARSA / Enicar / others

Highest-value evidence:
- A. Schild technical drawings / prototype date
- contemporary catalog / advertisement
- patent / design filing
- ARSA catalog or factory record
- AFB purchase order / supplier correspondence / invoice
- caseback / case-maker marks from surviving examples

### WEB-CONFIRMED — AFB wartime repair evidence

American Printing House for the Blind (APH), which holds the AFB archive, reports:

- AFB operated a free braille-watch repair service
- one archive folder contains hundreds of repair receipts
- an AFB repairman reported that many watches returned because tobacco flakes entered the open face and clogged the works
- correspondence survives between a caseworker and Katherine Gruber, director of AFB's braille-watch program

Source:
https://www.aph.org/blog/the-gift-of-time/

**Implication:** an open-face / touch-access watch has a documented contamination pathway in real use. This does not prove the same failure rate for ARSA's hinged-lid design.

### NOT PROVEN

- finger pressure commonly propagates into the train and breaks gears
- ARSA's hinge pin is a known high-frequency failure
- rarity today is primarily caused by tactile-use damage
- blind-alarm watches fail more often than ordinary alarm watches

Keep these as engineering hypotheses until evidence appears.

## 8. Track B — tactile-watch / tactile-alarm history

### WEB-CONFIRMED — prewar institutional base in the United States

AFB official history states that in **1926** the Foundation took over a watch-accommodation service previously handled by the Matilda Ziegler Magazine for the Blind. An arrangement initially involving **Waltham Watch Company** allowed blind people to obtain reliable watches at low cost. This became the nucleus of AFB's Aids and Appliances Division.

By 1972 the division's sales included roughly 300 products, including **30 styles of brailled pocket and wrist watches**.

Source:
https://afb.org/online-library/unseen-minority-0/chapter-7

**Implication:** AFB had a long-standing watch-procurement / distribution infrastructure well before the ARSA Blind Alarm period.

### WEB-CONFIRMED — WWII acceleration in the United States

AFB official history:

- AFB began presenting braille watches to blinded servicemen during WWII
- 1943 annual-meeting rationale framed learning to tell time as an early step in readjustment
- an initial watch order required **$8,000**
- by 1950 AFB had presented **1,464** blinded servicemen with personalized braille watches
- wartime civilian watch shortages led to public appeals for used watches, which were cleaned, repaired, and modified with tactile dots
- servicemen who had lost both sight and hands could not use tactile watches; specially adapted repeater watches based on a St Dunstan's concept were used instead
- the practice continued through the Korean War and early Vietnam era
- AFB stopped the program in 1963 after the VA could supply such devices

Source:
https://www.afb.org/about-afb/history/online-library/unseen-minority/chapter-17

APH archive summary adds:
- by Dec. 1945, **1,048** braille watches had been delivered
- by 1959, another **87** had been given to veterans blinded in the Korean War
- some watches were sent to POWs
- repair documentation survives in quantity

Source:
https://www.aph.org/blog/the-gift-of-time/

### WEB-CONFIRMED — WWI / St Dunstan's / Blind Veterans UK

Blind Veterans UK:
- organization founded in **1915** to support veterans blinded in WWI
- by end of 1918, more than **1,500 names** were on its books
- archive material explicitly preserves braille watches
- current archive description states founder Sir Arthur Pearson visited blinded men and gave each a watch as a demonstration that blindness did not eliminate independent action

Sources:
https://www.blindveterans.org.uk/about-us/who-we-are/our-history/
https://www.blindveterans.org.uk/about-us/who-we-are/our-history/our-archives/
https://www.blindveterans.org.uk/news/110-years-of-blind-veterans-uk/

### WEB-CONFIRMED — Japan

Seiko Museum Ginza:
- 1939 Seikosha pocket watch for blind users
- front lid opened by push-button
- time read by touching hands and raised dial projections
- during wartime, servicemen blinded in military service were presented with the watch
- at least seven presentation occasions are recorded through 1944
- about 20 recipients per occasion is the museum's current estimate

Source:
https://museum.seiko.co.jp/en/collections/watch_previousterm/collect044/
Japanese page:
https://museum.seiko.co.jp/collections/watch_previousterm/collect044/

### Historical model — INFERENCE, currently best-supported

Current evidence supports this sequence better than “war directly created the blind alarm”:

1. tactile / blind watches existed before the 1950s
2. WWI and especially WWII created large cohorts of war-blinded people
3. rehabilitation organizations, charities, military and veterans systems institutionalized supply, training, repair and distribution of tactile watches
4. by the postwar period, tactile watches were an established assistive-device category with procurement networks
5. mechanical alarm-wristwatch technology matured rapidly in the 1950s
6. around 1956–60, several firms applied alarm technology to tactile-watch architecture

Therefore:

> **War demand appears to have massively expanded and institutionalized tactile timekeeping. Blind Alarm is better treated as a postwar second-stage combination of that established tactile-watch infrastructure with 1950s alarm-watch technology, not as a direct wartime invention.**

### Important limit

No primary source currently found says:

- “ARSA created its Blind Alarm because of war veterans”
- “AFB commissioned ARSA specifically for veterans”
- “the 1950s Blind Alarm wave was directly caused by WWII demand”

Those causal links remain **OPEN** until documentary evidence is found.

### 2026-10-01 — parallel follow-up: pre-ARSA tactile architecture, wartime alarm research, and contemporary failure evidence

#### Track A / mechanism origin — earlier tactile-watch architecture found

**WEB-CONFIRMED / specialist Web lead:** URSS Watch documents a 1938 First Moscow Watch Factory Type-1 blind wristwatch with:
- no seconds hand
- raised tactile dial
- reinforced hands
- a push-button in the crown that releases the crystal, which opens under spring action

Sources:
- https://urss.watch/2022/12/04/type-1-pour-aveugle-1fmm/
- https://urss.watch/2021/07/27/les-montres-pour-aveugles/

**Research consequence:** the crown-integrated push-button + opening crystal + reinforced tactile hands architecture predates ARSA's circa-1956 Blind Alarm by roughly two decades. Therefore:
- do **not** describe ARSA as inventor of the crown-pusher opening architecture
- do **not** use the 1950s Swiss Blind Alarm cluster as the origin point of tactile wristwatch construction
- the 1950s innovation question should be narrowed to the **integration of tactile time-reading with a wrist-alarm function**, not the tactile case architecture itself

The 1938 Soviet example is a strong counterexample but is not yet a factory primary document; primary Soviet catalog / archive confirmation remains desirable.

#### Track B / WWI industrialization — Waltham touch-readable watch patent

**WEB-CONFIRMED / patent register:** Waltham Watch Co. was assigned U.S. Patent **US1222369A, Watch Readable By Touch**, issued 1917-04-10, inventor Harrie E. Duncan.

Sources:
- https://pocketwatchdatabase.com/guide/company/waltham/patents
- patent lineage references: https://patents.google.com/patent/US20050135198

**Research consequence:** touch-readable timepieces were an active industrial design problem during WWI itself, not merely a later charitable adaptation. This strengthens the model:
WWI mass casualty / rehabilitation demand + industrial watchmaking capability -> institutionalization of tactile timekeeping.

It does **not** prove the Waltham patent was created specifically for war-blinded veterans unless the patent / company archive says so.

#### Track A / failure mode — 1957 patent explicitly identifies damage from touching hands

**WEB-CONFIRMED / patent:** Edgar A. Ferguson Jr., **US2915874A, Tactile Indicators**, filed 1957-08-26, states that existing tactile watches required the user to touch the watch hands directly and that doing so could disturb the setting; the German family **DE1147393B** goes further in translation, stating direct touch can disturb the movement or bend the hands.

Sources:
- https://patents.google.com/patent/US2915874
- https://patents.google.com/patent/DE1147393B/en

The proposed solution used tactile magnetic elements rather than directly exposed hands.

**Research consequence:** hand disturbance / hand bending is not only a modern repair anecdote. It was a documented design problem contemporaneous with the ARSA / Enicar Blind Alarm era.

This upgrades the failure map:
- **direct-contact hand disturbance / bending** -> supported as a historically recognized design problem
- **gear-train breakage caused by finger pressure** -> still NOT PROVEN
- **ARSA-specific hand-failure frequency** -> still OPEN

#### Track B / wartime assistive-alarm research — AFB 1944 vibrating alarm clock

**WEB-CONFIRMED / official AFB:** AFB's 1944 research laboratory created and distributed assistive devices including a **vibrating alarm clock**. AFB states that by the end of the 1940s it had distributed more than 30,000 devices from this broader program.

Source:
- https://afb.org/afb100/our-history
- https://www.afb.org/about-afb/history/afb-timeline

**Research consequence:** during WWII, AFB was working not only on tactile time-reading but also on non-visual / non-auditory alarm signaling in the wider assistive-device program.

Important limit:
- this was an alarm **clock**, not a wristwatch
- no current evidence connects this vibrating clock technically to ARSA / AS 1475
- the 30,000 figure refers to the wider device program, not 30,000 alarm clocks

#### Updated two-track interpretation

**Track A:** the specific research novelty of the 1950s Blind Alarm cluster is increasingly the combination of:
- established tactile-watch architecture
- mechanical alarm movement
- tactile alarm-setting indication
- differentiated crowns / controls

rather than invention of the hinged tactile watch itself.

**Track B:** the historical chain now has firmer separate nodes:
- 1917 industrial touch-readable watch patent (Waltham)

#### TERMINOLOGY — “Braille watch” is often a historical/common label for a tactile analog watch, not literal Braille numerals

AFB's AccessWorld describes traditional “braille watches” as watches with a **hinged crystal**, raised hour markers, and hour/minute hands read by direct touch.

A later US Braille-watch patent explicitly distinguishes the existing **tactile watch** — exposed hands plus raised markings — from its proposed system that actually forms Braille numbers with pins. It notes that tactile-watch hour markings have no standard format.

Sources:
- https://afb.org/aw/20/1/14989
- https://patents.google.com/patent/US8483018B2/en

The current ARSA Blind Watch site itself uses both “blind” and “braille” terminology while describing raised markers and hands under a flip-open crystal:
- https://blindwatch.net/

**VA terminology consequence:** historical labels such as *Braille watch* may be retained when they belong to a source, market or institution, but VINTAGE ALARM should not imply that the vintage ARSA dial encodes time as Braille numerals. Mechanically and ergonomically it is a **tactile analog interface**.

#### ACCESSIBILITY LOOP — the alarm complication is tactile at the input / read-back side, not only audible at the output side

Horlbeck's Enicar Blind Alarm description is especially important because it states that the blind user can feel both:
- the current clock time
- the **set alarm time**, and set it again

The Enicar comparison specimen uses deliberately differentiated pointers:
- minute hand: about 1.6 mm wide
- hour hand: about 2.9 mm wide
- alarm hand: about 0.7 mm wide with four ridges at its end
- enlarged / differentiated hour markers, especially at 3 / 6 / 9

Project source:
- *The Alarm Wrist Watch*, “Special Watches” / Enicar blind alarm section.

**Boundary:** these exact dimensions and ridges are Enicar-specific evidence. Do not transfer them to the ARSA specimen without direct ARSA evidence.

**Research consequence:** the strongest way to explain the category is not merely “an alarm watch a blind person can hear.” The interface aims to make the **alarm setting itself tactilely inspectable**. That turns an ordinary alarm complication into a three-pointer tactile information system.
